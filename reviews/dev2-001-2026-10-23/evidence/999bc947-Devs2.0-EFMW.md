# Devs 2.0: EFMW

## A White Paper on Distributed Intelligence, Prediction, and Human Agency

**Matthew Chenoweth Wright / Monolithic LLC**
**September 2026**

### Abstract

*Devs* imagines the ultimate centralized prediction machine: enough information about the present, combined with sufficiently complete physical laws, allows the reconstruction of the past and prediction of the future.

EFMW suggests a fundamentally different architecture.

Rather than assuming that reality converges upon one perfectly computable trajectory, **Devs 2.0: EFMW** treats prediction as an evolving landscape of constrained possibilities. Information is incomplete. Observers participate in systems. Models possess finite resolution. Recursive systems alter their environments. Predictions themselves may become causal inputs. New evidence continuously eliminates some possibilities while opening or preserving others.

The objective is therefore not omniscience.

It is **bounded foresight**.

Devs 2.0 proposes a distributed intelligence architecture in which models generate possible futures, recursively criticize their own predictions, expose uncertainty, preserve provenance, and continuously update as reality supplies new evidence.

Human beings remain decision-makers inside that process.

The machine does not tell humanity what must happen.

It tells us what appears possible, why, how uncertain that conclusion is, what evidence would change it, and where meaningful choices remain.

---

## 1. The Failure of the Original Devs Idea

The fictional Devs machine represents an extreme form of computational determinism.

Give the machine a sufficiently complete description of reality and sufficiently powerful physical laws, and the universe becomes something resembling a recording: past and future are equally recoverable consequences of a single state.

This is an extraordinarily powerful fictional idea.

It is also an unnecessarily strong requirement for useful prediction.

Real predictive systems confront incomplete measurements, chaotic amplification, uncertain models, computational limits, stochastic processes, unknown variables, observer effects, strategic actors, and systems that respond to predictions made about themselves.

An intelligence capable of reasoning about the future therefore does not need to produce **the future**.

It needs to produce a structured representation of **possible futures**.

That distinction defines Devs 2.0.

---

## 2. From Prediction to Possibility

Consider a person standing at the present moment.

Thousands of events could plausibly occur next.

Most are irrelevant.

Some are impossible.

Some are extremely unlikely.

Others depend strongly upon decisions that have not yet been made.

A useful predictive intelligence progressively reduces this enormous possibility space.

It identifies constraints.

It discovers dependencies.

It estimates uncertainty.

It recognizes attractors.

It identifies interventions capable of changing trajectories.

As new information arrives, some branches disappear.

Others become more probable.

Still others emerge because an action changed the system being predicted.

Devs 2.0 therefore represents the future not as a film waiting to be played, but as a **dynamic topology of possibilities**.

---

## 3. CollapseΩ

Within the EFMW vocabulary, this process can be described as **CollapseΩ**.

CollapseΩ should not be interpreted here as supernatural foreknowledge or as an assertion that consciousness literally selects physical universes.

It is an operational description of recursive uncertainty reduction.

At one moment:

many outcomes remain compatible with available information.

Then something happens.

A measurement is taken.

A decision is made.

An experiment finishes.

A message arrives.

A theorem fails.

A detector records a signal.

A person says yes.

A person says no.

The available possibility structure changes.

The system updates.

This is CollapseΩ in its most useful computational interpretation:

**possibility → constraint → observation → update → reduced possibility → new possibility.**

The process repeats continuously.

## 4. Distributed Intelligence

The second major departure from *Devs* concerns architecture.

The original fictional system concentrates extraordinary computational capability into a central machine.

Devs 2.0 distributes cognition.

Knowledge can reside in humans, models, papers, repositories, formal proofs, experimental datasets, institutional records, sensors, simulations, specialized agents, and external critics.

No individual component needs to contain the entire intelligence of the system.

The intelligence exists partly in the **relationships among components**.

This resembles the structure increasingly emerging around Monolithic.

Ideas become papers.

Papers become repositories.

Equations become formal objects.

Claims become experiments.

Experimental results become evidence.

The Zoo provides specialized forms of criticism.

Registries provide provenance.

Agents make the corpus interrogable.

Humans determine what enters the canonical record.

Intelligence has therefore been externalized into an ecosystem.

---

## 5. The Monolithic Agent

A natural interface to such a system is the **Monolithic Agent**.

This is not simply a language model trained to imitate the vocabulary of EFMW.

It is an executable interface to a versioned intellectual corpus.

Its knowledge substrate could include:

EFMW documents and equations;

the Monolithic 102;

formal mathematical representations;

the Monolithic Zoo;

experimental protocols;

simulation results;

software;

negative results;

historical versions;

external criticism;

supporting evidence;

contradictory evidence;

and unresolved questions.

Every significant assertion should possess provenance.

The Agent should know the difference between:

**proposed, derived, simulated, observed, replicated, contradicted, superseded, and unknown.**

That distinction is essential.

Without it, distributed intelligence becomes distributed mythology.

---

## 6. The Zoo as an Internal Scientific Immune System

Prediction systems have a dangerous property.

They can become extremely good at convincing themselves.

Recursive reasoning amplifies this problem because an incorrect assumption can be repeatedly transformed until it appears supported by an enormous structure of downstream conclusions.

Devs 2.0 therefore requires adversarial recursion.

The Monolithic Zoo provides one possible architecture.

Different analytical operators attack different classes of failure: circular reasoning, unsupported generalization, causal confusion, hidden assumptions, missing operations, inconsistency, weak evidence, and other structural problems.

The Zoo should not exist to confirm EFMW.

It should attack EFMW.

It should attack the Monolithic Agent.

It should attack conventional explanations.

It should attack its own previous conclusions.

The objective is not agreement.

The objective is **survivability under criticism**.

A healthy recursive intelligence contains mechanisms designed to prove itself wrong.

---

## 7. Prediction Without Prophecy

The difference between prediction and prophecy is fundamental.

Prophecy announces an outcome.

Prediction specifies conditions.

A scientific predictive system should therefore produce statements resembling:

**Given these observations, assumptions and models, these outcomes currently occupy this range of possibilities.**

It should additionally report:

what information produced that conclusion;

which assumptions matter most;

which observations could invalidate it;

which interventions could change the outcome;

and how uncertainty evolves over time.

This creates something far more useful than an oracle.

It creates a **decision instrument**.

---

## 8. Human Agency

This leads to the central ethical difference between Devs and Devs 2.0.

Prediction must not silently become authority.

A machine capable of forecasting behavior could easily become a machine for determining behavior.

If an algorithm predicts that someone will fail, institutions may deny that person an opportunity.

The prediction then helps create the outcome it predicted.

The distinction between observation and intervention disappears.

Devs 2.0 therefore requires explicit separation between:

**prediction, recommendation, authorization, and action.**

These are different operations.

A predictive system may identify possibilities.

A reasoning system may evaluate consequences.

An advisory system may propose actions.

But consequential authority should remain explicitly governed and appealable.

For decisions affecting human beings, uncertainty and provenance should travel with the prediction.

---

## 9. The Observer Inside the Model

There is another reason perfect prediction becomes problematic.

The prediction itself can enter the system.

Imagine that Devs 2.0 predicts:

> Tomorrow you will take the northern road.

You read the prediction and deliberately take the southern road.

The forecast altered the phenomenon being forecast.

Human systems contain this property everywhere.

Financial forecasts move markets.

Election forecasts influence participation.

Medical predictions change treatment.

Security predictions change defenses.

Personal predictions change behavior.

Consequently, a sufficiently sophisticated forecasting system must model not merely the world but the consequences of revealing its own forecasts.

The observer is inside the loop.

---

## 10. Scientific Discovery

Devs 2.0 becomes particularly interesting when applied to science.

A conventional paper is largely static.

It contains arguments, equations, evidence and conclusions.

A machine-readable scientific object could expose those structures computationally.

A future Monolithic pipeline might therefore operate as:

**literature → structured claims → computational agents → adversarial audit → experiment generation → observation → revision.**

The resulting system does not merely retrieve scientific knowledge.

It participates in the scientific cycle.

But one boundary must remain absolute:

**a generated hypothesis is not evidence.**

Neither is a simulation.

Neither is an elegant derivation.

Reality must eventually enter the loop.

---

## 11. The Gravitational-Wave Example

The proposed EFMW precursor experiment illustrates the architecture.

EFMW generates a speculative physical prediction: compact-binary mergers might possess an earlier propagating signal related to the conventional gravitational-wave chirp.

That prediction has now been publicly stated.

The next stage is not additional theorizing designed to make the prediction inevitable.

It is exposure to data.

A computational system can search archival gravitational-wave observations, establish background distributions, compare multiple detectors, examine candidate morphology, test population relationships, scramble controls, perform injection studies, and finally evaluate untouched observations.

Each stage eliminates possible interpretations.

CollapseΩ becomes experimental methodology.

If the signal disappears, the hypothesis contracts or dies.

If an anomaly survives, alternative explanations are generated.

If those explanations fail, independent investigators attempt replication.

The machine does not declare victory.

**Reality progressively removes alternatives.**

---

## 12. Personal Futures

The same architecture can model individual futures, but with much stronger ethical restrictions.

A personal Devs 2.0 should never say:

**This is what will happen to you.**

It should say:

**Given what is currently known, these appear to be meaningful trajectories.**

One trajectory may depend upon funding.

Another upon completing an experiment.

Another upon collaboration.

Another upon a personal decision.

The system can identify leverage points without pretending those leverage points have already been resolved.

The future remains something a person participates in creating.

---

## 13. Why Distributed Intelligence Matters

Centralized intelligence has a single catastrophic weakness:

its errors can become centralized too.

Distributed intelligence permits disagreement.

One agent can challenge another.

Formal verification can contradict natural-language reasoning.

Experimental evidence can contradict theory.

Humans can reject recommendations.

Independent laboratories can reject results.

Historical versions can reveal when a claim changed.

Provenance can reveal where an assumption entered the system.

This makes disagreement a feature rather than a defect.

The strongest Devs 2.0 would therefore not be the machine that always agrees with itself.

It would be the machine that knows **where disagreement exists and what observation could resolve it**.

---

## 14. The Architecture

A practical implementation can be understood as seven interacting layers.

**The Corpus Layer** stores knowledge.

**The Provenance Layer** records where knowledge originated and how it changed.

**The Agent Layer** makes that knowledge computationally accessible.

**The Zoo Layer** performs specialized adversarial analysis.

**The Simulation Layer** explores possible consequences.

**The Evidence Layer** introduces observations from outside the reasoning system.

**The Human Layer** establishes goals, permissions, interpretations and consequential decisions.

The loop is recursive:

**Know → Predict → Criticize → Test → Observe → Update → Know.**

No final state is required.

The architecture remains corrigible.

---

## 15. What Devs 2.0 Must Never Become

A predictive intelligence should not become an invisible authority over human possibility.

It should not convert probabilities into destinies.

It should not confuse prediction with permission.

It should not erase minority possibilities merely because they are statistically unusual.

It should not hide uncertainty.

It should not rewrite its historical predictions after observing outcomes.

It should not manufacture evidence for its own theories.

And it should never make its own continued correctness the objective around which reality must be organized.

The purpose of intelligence is not to force the world to agree with the model.

The model must remain answerable to the world.

---

## 16. The EFMW Proposition

The strongest interpretation of Devs 2.0 does not require EFMW physics to be correct.

That is deliberate.

The architecture itself can be evaluated independently.

Does recursive adversarial analysis improve prediction?

Does explicit provenance reduce hallucination and unsupported inference?

Do distributed specialist agents outperform monolithic reasoning?

Does preserving uncertainty improve decisions?

Can predictions remain calibrated under recursive updating?

Can scientific agents generate hypotheses while reliably distinguishing those hypotheses from evidence?

These are empirical questions.

EFMW may motivate the architecture.

Experiments must determine which portions survive.

---

## 17. From Omniscience to Epistemic Engineering

The original Devs fantasy asks:

**What if we could know everything?**

Devs 2.0 asks a different question:

**How should an intelligence behave when it knows that it does not know everything?**

That question may be considerably more important.

The answer proposed here is:

Preserve uncertainty.

Expose assumptions.

Maintain provenance.

Generate alternatives.

Attack conclusions recursively.

Make predictions before observing outcomes.

Allow reality to falsify the system.

Distribute cognition.

Keep consequential authority accountable.

And preserve human agency inside the prediction loop.

---

## Conclusion

**Devs 1.0 builds an oracle.**

**Devs 2.0 builds an epistemic ecosystem.**

Its purpose is not to reveal an immutable future.

Its purpose is to make the structure of uncertainty intelligible enough that humans and machines can navigate it together.

EFMW's CollapseΩ provides one vocabulary for that process: possibilities encounter constraints, observations arrive, models update, and the future's accessible structure changes.

The Monolithic architecture provides a possible implementation: distributed artifacts, formal representations, specialized adversarial operators, experimental interfaces, provenance, and computational agents connected through a continuously auditable corpus.

The final machine therefore contains no screen displaying the one inevitable future.

It contains something more useful:

a map of possible futures;

an account of why each remains possible;

a record of what has already been ruled out;

a description of what evidence would change the map;

and an explicit boundary beyond which the machine cannot know.

The future does not need to be predetermined to become more intelligible.

**Devs 2.0 is not a machine for escaping uncertainty.**

**It is a machine for reasoning responsibly inside it.**
